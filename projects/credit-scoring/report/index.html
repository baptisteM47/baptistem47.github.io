An additional explanation is the way the data set is constructed. For “date_jugement” and “nature_jugement” this is expected as most of the companies do not default. Also, it has no impact on our analysis as we only use those observation to create the default variable.

However, for other variables this is concerning. To address it we decide to first keep only the non-default companies who have less missing values. Of course, it assumes that the number of NAs is independent of the company’s characteristics. We choose a low quantile (5% of the non-defaulting companies with less NAs) such that the data is cleaner and it also help us to rebalance our data as the share of defaulting firms mechanically increases. Then, on the remaining companies, we only keep variables with less than 25% missing values, which we replace by their respective median. We use a threshold as it would make no sense to replace missing values by the median if a variable has to many NAs.

Keep 5% best non-default companies + 25% threshold

At first we wanted to remove the rows with the more NAs. However this could create inconsistency. For instance, if a company has a lot of NAs during one year there would be a year with no observation for this company. Hence, we decide to remove directly the non-default firms which across our period of interest have the more NAs.

We first proceed to select the best 5% non-default companies:

Create the ratios and financial indicators

We now create our financial indicators and ratios using only the remaining variables. Recall that when we created the dataset, we kept only some keys in “liasse” that were used for the ratios on La Loupe Financière (2024). But since, we removed some variables when dealing with the NAs, we make a code such that only ratios which use the remaining variables are constructed. For that, we build the function “safe_mutate” with the help of chat GPT.

#Safe mutate function: only creates variable if all required variables exist.
safe_mutate <- function(data, name, vars, expr) {
  if (all(vars %in% colnames(data))) {
    data %>% mutate(!!name := !!parse_expr(expr))
  } else data
}

#Convert numeric columns and arrange
DF_Ratios <- DF_FULL_NONAS %>%
  mutate(across(all_of(numeric_cols), as.numeric)) %>%
  arrange(siren, date_cloture_exercice)

#We construct the intermediate variables
DF_Ratios <- DF_Ratios %>%
  safe_mutate("CHIFFRE_AFFAIRE", "FL", "FL") %>% 
  safe_mutate("RESULTAT_FINANCIER", c("GP","GU"), "GP - GU") %>%
  safe_mutate("TOTAL_ACTIF", c("CO","1A"), "CO - `1A`") %>%
  safe_mutate("VA", c("FL","FM","FN","FS","FT","FU","FV","FW","HP","HQ"),

  safe_mutate("BFR", c("CJ","CK","CF","CG","CD","CE","DW","DX","DY","DZ","EA","EB"),

According to the results obtained, we have a decreasing relationship between the default event and the level of equity. We can also observe that as the level of depreciation expenses increases the probability of defaulting drops. This result makes sense as a higher level of depreciation expenses is often related to a higher level of assets (machinery, buildings etc).

Conversely, we can also observe that the probability of defaulting increases when the financial result becomes positive. This could be because financial income may only reflect exceptional or temporary income, particularly in the hospitality and restaurant sector. This is because financial income in this sector is often temporary and unrelated to its core activities.

We also find that when commercial margin ratio becomes positive, the probability of default increases slightly. At the first sight it can be counter-intuitive, but when we look closely to how the ratio is constructed and we remarked that this ratio depends negatively with the purchase of goods and variations of goods. In the specific sector of hospitality and restaurants typically experience large variations in purchases of goods and inventories due to demand fluctuations of their customer base.

Additionally, as the gross margin ratio increases, the probability of default rate decreases. The gross margin ratio is computed by its operating Income divided by its commercial margin. This result make sense as the operating income relates to the profit generated by the core activities of the businesses before the taxes and financial income.

There is a negative relationship between the net margin ratio and the number of defaulting businesses. Indeed the net margin ratio is constructed by dividing the net results by its turnover. In other words, this ratio computes the how much profit is generated per unit of revenue.

An increase of the customer credit duration ratio (measured in days) is associated with more defaulting businesses. This ratio is composed by accounts receivable and its depreciation divided by its turnover. A longer customer credit duration could indicate slower collection of receivables, which weakens liquidity and increases the probability of default of the business.

Finally, we can also note that the exportation ratio, constructed from the overall level of exportation and the turnover, has a positive relationship with the probability of default. In the specific sector of hospitality and restaurant, a possible reason for this results is that a higher level of exportation may be associated with greater risks (exchange rate and geopolitical risks).

Section 2: Analysis

Our strategy is to first train each model on a training set (2017-2019), then we obtain the parameters using the validation set (2020), afterwards we refit the model with its appropriate parameters on training+validation set (to gain some observations) and finally, we compare our models on testing set (2021). See Moody’s methodology.

Section 2.1 LASSO

We decided to run a Lasso regression model, following the Moody’s methodology, to divide the data into training, validation and testing sets. Since we have a time series model, we divided our database into years. The training set comprises the years 2017 to 2019; the validation set, which is used to determine the optimal lambda for the Lasso, comprises the year 2020; and the testing set comprises the year 2021, which is used to evaluate our regressions.

For this first Lasso model, we found that the optimal lambda was 6.8e-05, with an AUC of 0.96 on the training set, 0.90 on the validation set, and 0.85 on the testing set, which is excellent. Looking at the difference in the AUC between the training set and the validation set, we can see that there is some overfitting. Nevertheless, this gap is not too big, since we trained our model on three years’ worth of data and validated and tested on one year’s worth of data for each. Nevertheless, we can conclude that, with an AUC of 0.85, we have a good model.

Examining the Lasso coefficients reveals that certain variables have a more significant impact on the probability of default than others. For example, the ‘export rate’, ‘net margin’ and ‘commercial margin’ have a positive impact of high magnitude on the probability of default, compared to other variables. However, this is not very coherent since ‘net margin’ and ‘commercial margin’ are directly linked to the company’s financial health. Nevertheless, this effect can be biased by the size of turnover since these variables are divided by it. Conversely, the impact of the ‘export rate’ may also be biased, as its size could be due to low turnover. Since exports are also divided by it, this increases the value of the coefficient. Therefore, the impact of the ‘export rate’ is difficult to interpret. Furthermore, some variables have a negative impact on the probability of default, such as the ‘exploitation margin’ and the ‘real estate turnover’ ratio. This is coherent, as a large exploitation margin indicates that a company is not likely to be in financial distress. Conversely, a company with a lot of real estate assets can sell them if it is in financial distress, so it is also unlikely to be in financial distress if it holds real estate assets. Nevertheless, as with the ‘export rate’, it is difficult to interpret this variable since it is divided by the turnover.

Looking at the odds ratio, we can see that an increase of one unit in ‘export_rate’ multiplies the odds of default by more than four. This confirms our concerns about the construction of these variables and their real effect on company defaults. Furthermore, we can see that an increase of one unit in the ‘net margin’ multiplies the odds of default by 1.8, this also confirms that the result is not very coherent. Regarding the negatively correlated coefficient, an increase of one unit in the ‘real estate turnover’ ratio decreases the odds of default by almost 5%, whereas an increase of one unit in the ‘exploitation margin’ decreases the odds of default by 79%.

Since these are ratio or margin variables, an increase of one unit does not correspond to a 1% increase. This is why the increases and decreases in odds are so high.

Section 2.3 Gradient Boosting

The following code allows us to test the GBM model using several combinations of six different parameters inluding the number of boosting iterations (the number of trees), the learning rate, the depth of each tree, minimum child weight, susbsample ratio and column subsample ratio.

According to https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/parameter.html#parameters-for-tree-booster,

  • Learning rate (eta) : corresponds to the step size shrinkage for each new tree.

  • Depth of each tree (max_depth): corresponds to the maximum size of the tree, as the tree depth increases, this could lead to a more complex model and more risk of over fitting.

  • Minimum child weight (min_child_weight): controls the minimum sum of instance weights (similar to observation counts) required in a child node for a split to be allowed (reduce the risk of overfitting).

  • Subsample ratio (subsample): controls for the fraction of the training observations used to grow each tree.

  • Column subsample ratio (colsample_bytree): defines the fraction of features ( columns) considered when construction each tree.

Firstly, the model is tested on the validation set for all possible combinations in order to select the combination of parameters that yields the highest AUC. Secondly, once the best combination has been found, the model is retrained on the training set + validation set and finally assessed on the testing set using a ROC curve, as done earlier in the decision tree Section.

Section 3 Conclusion

The aim of this credit scoring project is to assess the performance of several machine learning models to predict the default of companies using a data-analysis approach. The project comprises three main parts: data collection, data exploration, and finally data analysis. Our project relies on three different databases: BODACC data to identify defaulting and non-defaulting companies; INPI data to collect financial data of each company; and NAF data to gain insight into each company’s sector of activity. For this credit scoring project, we have decided to focus our analysis on the “Accommodation and catering” sector from 2017 to 2021. According to economic literature, this sector was considered the most impacted by the pandemic, resulting in a significant drop in cash flows. However, we found that this sector received substantial financial support from the French government. The interaction between the pandemic and this heavily impacted sector makes the study even more interesting to analyze.

For the data exploration stage, we decided to plot various graphs to familiarize ourselves with the data and potentially discover initial relationships between the default event and financial variables. During this process, we discovered that several variables had many extreme and missing values. Therefore, we retained the top 5% of non-defaulting firms with the fewest missing values, dropped variables with more than 25% missing observations and imputed the remaining missing data using medians. We also winsorised the variables (5th-95th percentile). Additionally, bivariate plots showed that higher turnover is associated with lower default, while longer customer credit durations (in days) are associated with a higher default probability.

We also conducted a Principal Component Analysis (PCA), but unfortunately this did not produce a good enough score to distinguish between the two classes. However, we conducted a Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) and achieved good performance on the validation set, with an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 75.8%.

Finally, our project also involved data analysis, for which we trained and assessed several models: Lasso (full set of variables), Lasso (ratios only), Decision Tree and XGBoost. We always trained our models on a training set, select our parameters on the validation set, retrain the model with the selected parameters on the merged training and validation set, then we test the models on a testing set.

Firstly, we estimated two Lasso models: one containing all the financial variables and another containing only the financial variables we created (mostly the ratios). We used the validation set to select the optimal penalty parameters for these models. Both models performed well on the testing set (AUC = 0.85 for the full model and = 0.824 for the ratio-only model). The data analysis section also involved decision tree models. We created a loop to test several combinations of parameters (cp and minsplit) and selected the best one (higher AUC), and we get an AUC with the best combinaison on the testing set of 88.23%.

Finally, we estimated an XGBoost model. Here, we created a loop to test all possible combinations of six different parameters, including the number of boosting iterations, the learning rate, the depth of each tree, minimum child weight, subsample ratio, and column subsample ratio. This resulted in 540 different models. Using the validation set, we selected the combination of parameters that yielded the highest AUC, and after retraining the model on training and validation set, we tested the model on the testing set, achieving an AUC of 95.8%.

In conclusion, the XGboost model is the most performant, with an AUC of 95.8%.

However, despite these strong predictive results, limitations should be acknowledged. Due to our significant data imbalance, ROC-AUC can be misleading. For instance, a model may allocate slightly higher risk scores to firms that default than to those that do not (yielding a high ROC-AUC), yet still predict that almost all firms will not default. Consequently, there are very few detected defaults, resulting in poor practical usefulness. Therefore, including other metrics in our analysis could greatly improve it.
